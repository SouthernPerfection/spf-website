metal finishing & powder coating: the complete guide.

A practical, vendor-neutral guide to metal finishing, powder coating and industrial painting services — how each process works, which coating chemistries fit which environments, why surface preparation makes or breaks a finish, and how to choose a coating that lasts.

Start an RFQ →Powder coating services

Introduction

Why metal finishing matters.

Metal finishing is the final — and often most underrated — step in manufacturing. It is what stands between raw fabricated steel and a part that survives years of weather, chemicals, abrasion and handling. A finish is not just cosmetic: it is the primary corrosion barrier, the difference between a bracket that rusts in one wet season and one that looks new after a decade. When a coating fails, it rarely fails because the paint or powder was bad. It fails because the surface underneath was not prepared correctly, or because the wrong chemistry was chosen for the environment the part actually lives in.

This guide walks through the two dominant industrial finishing methods — powder coating and wet paint — plus the surface preparation that both depend on, how they compare, and how to match a finish to indoor, outdoor, chemical, UV and high-temperature service. Southern Perfection Fabrication runs both in-house powder coat and wet paint lines with among the largest ovens in the Southeast, so the examples here reflect how finishing is done in a real production shop rather than in theory. If you are specifying a coating for fabricated parts, weldments or assemblies, understanding these fundamentals will help you write a better print and get a part that lasts.

Powder coating

How powder coating works.

Powder coating is a dry finishing process. Instead of a liquid, finely ground pigment and resin particles are given an electrostatic charge and sprayed onto a grounded metal part. Because the powder carries an opposite charge to the part, it wraps around edges and clings evenly to the surface — including areas the spray gun never points directly at. The coated part then travels into a curing oven, where heat melts the powder into a continuous liquid film that flows out smooth and chemically cross-links into a hard, durable shell. This oven cure is why oven capacity matters: a shop can only powder coat parts that physically fit through its ovens, so large weldments and assemblies depend on having large-format powder coating capability on site.

Because no solvents evaporate and overspray can be reclaimed, powder coating is efficient and produces very little waste, which is part of why it has become the default finish for so much fabricated metal. The result is typically a thicker, tougher film than wet paint, with excellent resistance to chipping and abrasion.

Powder chemistries.

Not all powders are the same. The resin system determines how the finish performs:

  • TGIC polyester — the workhorse for outdoor use, with excellent UV and weather resistance so colors hold up in sunlight.
  • Epoxy — outstanding chemical and corrosion resistance and adhesion, but it chalks and fades in sunlight, so it belongs indoors.
  • Epoxy-polyester hybrid — blends epoxy's toughness with better appearance, a common choice for indoor equipment and appliances.
  • Urethane (polyurethane) — a smooth, hard, weather-resistant finish prized for its appearance and thin-film flow.

Powder coatings are available in the full RAL color system as well as custom color matches, and in a range of textures and glosses — high gloss, satin, matte, wrinkle, hammertone and coarse textures that hide surface imperfections. Choosing the right chemistry, color and texture together is how a finish ends up both looking right and lasting.

Wet paint

Wet paint & industrial painting services.

Wet paint — the basis of most industrial painting services — is the older, more flexible method. Liquid coating is sprayed onto the part and cured by air drying or by heat, depending on the chemistry. While powder coating has taken over much high-volume fabrication work, wet paint still wins in several situations. It can coat parts that are too large or too heat-sensitive to fit through a curing oven, it reaches deep recesses and interior cavities that electrostatic powder can struggle with, and it offers coating systems — such as multi-coat primer-and-topcoat builds — that are hard to replicate in powder.

Common industrial wet coatings include alkyd and epoxy primers, acrylic and polyurethane topcoats, and specialized systems for marine, structural and high-corrosion service. Wet paint also makes precise low-volume color matching and touch-up simpler. In a full-service shop, powder and wet paint are complementary: the part, its size, its color requirements and its service environment decide which line it runs down. Southern Perfection offers both, so the choice is driven by what is best for the part rather than by what a single-process shop happens to own.

Surface prep

Sandblasting & surface preparation.

Every coating — powder or wet — is only as good as the surface under it. Sandblasting, or abrasive blasting, is the most thorough way to prepare fabricated metal. Blast media is propelled at high velocity against the part to strip away rust, mill scale, weld spatter and old coatings, and to leave behind a clean, uniformly roughened profile. That profile — the microscopic peaks and valleys — is what the coating mechanically grips. Too smooth and the finish can peel; too aggressive and it can telegraph through a thin film.

Beyond blasting, good surface prep includes degreasing to remove oils, and chemical pretreatments such as iron or zinc phosphate that improve both adhesion and corrosion resistance before coating. Skipping or shortcutting prep is the number-one cause of premature coating failure. This is why Southern Perfection brings sandblasting and surface prep in-house alongside coating — a part that is fabricated, blasted, prepped and coated in one building has fewer handoffs where contamination and rework creep in. For heavy steel fabrication and large weldments, integrated blasting and coating is often the difference between a finish that lasts and one that has to be redone.

Comparison

Powder coat vs. wet paint.

Both produce a durable, attractive finish, but they trade off differently. Powder coating generally offers a thicker, harder, more chip- and abrasion-resistant film, uses no solvents, wastes little material, and cures fast — but it requires that the part fit through a curing oven and be conductive enough to hold a charge. Wet paint handles oversized and heat-sensitive parts, reaches complex internal geometry, and enables multi-coat systems and easy touch-up — but it typically cures slower, uses solvents, and can be less abrasion-resistant film-for-film.

Durability

Powder wins on chip and abrasion resistance.

Part size

Wet paint handles parts too big for an oven.

Geometry

Wet paint reaches deep recesses and cavities.

Efficiency

Powder reclaims overspray with little waste.

The practical answer is rarely "always one or the other." It is whichever fits the part's size, geometry, color and environment — which is exactly why having both processes and complete custom manufacturing under one roof matters.

Selection

Choosing a finish for your environment.

The single most important question is: where will this part live? Match the coating to the exposure, not the other way around.

  • Indoor — protected from sunlight and weather; epoxy or hybrid powders offer excellent toughness and value.
  • Outdoor / UV — sunlight fades and chalks the wrong coating; TGIC polyester or urethane resists UV so color and gloss hold up.
  • Chemical exposure — solvents, acids or wash-down environments favor epoxy chemistries and robust pretreatment for corrosion resistance.
  • High temperature — exhaust, engine or furnace-adjacent parts need specialty high-temp coatings rated for the operating range.
  • Oversized or heat-sensitive — very large weldments or assemblies that cannot go through an oven point toward wet industrial paint.

Getting this decision right up front — before the part is fabricated — lets you specify prep, chemistry, color and process together, and avoids the expensive lesson of a coating that was never suited to its environment.

Longevity

Durability & maintenance.

A correctly specified, properly prepared and fully cured finish can protect a part for many years with minimal upkeep. Durability comes down to three things done right: thorough surface preparation, the correct coating chemistry for the environment, and a complete cure. Get all three right and the finish resists corrosion, chipping, chemicals and fading. Get any one wrong and the coating becomes the weak link.

Maintenance is straightforward. Periodic cleaning with mild detergent removes contaminants that can hold moisture against the surface, and inspecting for chips or scratches lets you touch up small damage before rust can undercut the surrounding coating. On outdoor equipment, addressing nicks early is the cheapest form of corrosion control there is. Backed by an ISO 9001 quality system and CAGE 2W654 registration, Southern Perfection applies finishing as a controlled, documented step in the fabrication process rather than an afterthought — which is what makes a coating repeatable from the first part to the thousandth.

FAQ

Metal finishing — answered.

What is the difference between powder coating and wet paint?

Powder coating applies a dry, electrostatically charged powder that is baked into a hard, uniform film, while wet paint is sprayed as a liquid and cured by air or heat. Powder is generally tougher, thicker and more efficient; wet paint reaches recesses and very large or heat-sensitive parts that cannot go through an oven.

How durable is powder coating?

A properly prepared and cured powder coat is highly resistant to chipping, abrasion, corrosion, chemicals and fading. Durability depends heavily on surface preparation and choosing the right powder chemistry — polyester for outdoor UV exposure, epoxy for indoor chemical resistance — for the part's service environment.

What is sandblasting and why does it matter?

Sandblasting, or abrasive blasting, propels media against the metal to strip rust, mill scale and old coatings and to create a clean, textured profile the finish can grip. Surface preparation is the single biggest driver of coating adhesion and long-term durability; a great coating over a poorly prepped surface will still fail.

How do I choose the right finish for my environment?

Match the coating to the exposure. Outdoor and UV-heavy applications favor TGIC polyester; indoor chemical or corrosion exposure favors epoxy or hybrid; high-temperature parts need specialty high-temp coatings; and very large or heat-sensitive assemblies may be better suited to wet industrial paint.
