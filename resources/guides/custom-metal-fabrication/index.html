Overview

What is custom metal fabrication?

Custom metal fabrication is the build-to-print process of turning raw metal — sheet, plate, tube and bar stock — into finished parts and assemblies engineered to a specific design rather than pulled from a catalog. Where a stock part is bought as-is, a fabricated part starts from an engineer’s drawing or CAD model and is cut, shaped, joined and finished to match exactly what the application demands: the right dimensions, the right strength, the right tolerances and the right surface.

That distinction matters because most industrial hardware simply doesn’t exist off the shelf. A conveyor frame, an equipment guard, a weldment that bolts into a machine, a bracket that has to survive a specific load — each one is unique to its job, and fabrication is how that uniqueness gets built accurately and repeatably. Good custom metal fabrication combines several distinct processes into one controlled workflow, so a part that needs cutting, bending, machining, welding and coating moves through each step without losing its dimensions along the way.

This guide walks through the whole picture: the core processes, the materials, the path from a first prototype to full production, why doing it all under one roof beats juggling vendors, and how to pick a partner and write a request for quote that gets you an accurate price the first time.

Processes

The core processes.

Almost every fabricated part is the sum of a handful of processes performed in sequence. Understanding what each one does — and what it’s good at — makes it far easier to design parts that are practical to build and to read a quote intelligently.

Cutting is where most parts begin. Flat stock is cut to a profile using a laser or plasma cutter, which follows the CAD outline to produce clean, repeatable blanks with holes and features already in place. Laser cutting excels at tight, accurate detail in thinner material; plasma handles thicker plate economically. Both remove the slow, error-prone work of manual layout and give every downstream step a precise starting part.

Forming bends those flat blanks into three-dimensional shapes. On a press brake, a punch presses the metal into a die to create bends, flanges, channels and enclosures. Capacity matters here: a heavy press brake — up to 230 tons in our shop — lets thick plate and long parts be formed in a single hit, while plate rolling turns flat stock into cylinders and cones for tanks, chutes and rolled structures.

Machining removes material with cutting tools to hit features and tolerances that forming can’t. Where a part needs a precise bore, a flat sealing face, a threaded hole or a close-fitting shaft, CNC machining delivers it to tight tolerances — typically down to ±0.005″ — so mating parts assemble correctly every time.

Welding joins the pieces into a finished weldment. Manual MIG and TIG welding handle complex, one-off and structural work, while robotic welding brings speed and consistency to higher volumes. A shop with 30+ MIG stations plus FANUC robotic cells can flex between a single prototype and a repeating production run without changing the quality of the joint.

Finishing protects and completes the part. Powder coating — along with wet paint and other treatments — adds corrosion resistance, color and a durable surface; large powder ovens let big weldments be coated in one piece rather than in sections. Assembly is the final step, where fasteners, inserts, hardware and sub-components are added so the part ships ready to install rather than as a pile of pieces.

Materials

Materials: carbon, stainless and aluminum.

Material choice drives cost, weight, strength and how a part is built and finished. Three metals cover the large majority of custom work, and knowing when to reach for each avoids over-building and over-spending.

Carbon steel is the workhorse. It’s strong, weldable, widely available and the most economical choice for structural and industrial parts — frames, racks, guards, brackets and weldments. Because bare steel rusts, carbon-steel parts are almost always finished with powder coat or paint. When the priority is strength per dollar, steel fabrication is usually the right starting point.

Stainless steel resists corrosion without a coating, which makes it the default where hygiene, washdown or chemical exposure are in play — food and beverage, pharmaceutical, and outdoor or marine environments. Grades like 304 and 316 hold up to moisture and cleaning chemicals, and stainless keeps a clean appearance, though it costs more and is slower to machine and weld than carbon steel.

Aluminum is chosen when weight matters. At roughly a third the density of steel, it’s ideal for parts that move, get lifted, or need to reduce a vehicle’s mass, and it naturally resists corrosion. Alloys such as 6061 balance strength and workability well. The trade-offs are lower absolute strength than steel and welding that demands more care. Much of this arrives as sheet metal fabrication, where thin-gauge stock is cut, formed and joined into enclosures, panels and light assemblies. When in doubt, let the environment and the load — not habit — pick the metal.

Lifecycle

Prototype to production.

A well-run fabrication program treats a part’s life as a continuum, not a series of disconnected orders. It usually starts with a prototype: one or a few parts built to prove the design fits, functions and can actually be made. This is the cheapest moment to discover that a bend collides with a weld, a tolerance is unrealistic, or a feature adds cost for no benefit. In-house engineering — SolidWorks modeling and design-for-manufacturing review — turns those discoveries into drawing changes before any tooling or volume is committed.

Once the prototype is validated, the same part moves into a pilot or low-volume run, where fixtures, weld sequences and inspection routines are dialed in. From there it scales to production, where repeatability and throughput dominate: robotic welding cells, repeatable press-brake setups and documented inspection keep part number ten thousand identical to part number one. Keeping the prototype and the production run in the same shop means nothing gets “lost in translation” when volume ramps — the people who solved the problems build the parts.

Sourcing

One roof vs. multiple vendors.

Because fabrication is several processes chained together, buyers face a basic sourcing choice: split the work across specialist vendors, or run it through one shop that owns the whole chain. The multi-vendor route can look cheaper line by line, but the hidden costs add up. Parts get trucked from the cutter to the forming shop to the machinist to the welder to the coater, adding freight, transit time and handling damage at every hop. Worse, when a dimension is off, each vendor can point at the next — and no single company owns the outcome.

A one-roof shop collapses that chain. Cutting, forming, machining, welding, finishing and assembly happen in the same 100,000-square-foot building, on one purchase order, under one ISO 9001 quality system. Lead times shrink because parts don’t sit on trucks, quality is easier to control because one team is accountable end to end, and engineering can catch a conflict between, say, a formed flange and a welded joint before either is made. For anything that touches more than one or two processes, consolidation is usually the faster and lower-risk path.

Selection

How to choose a fabrication partner.

Not every shop can build every part, so match the partner to the work. A few things separate a dependable supplier from a risky one:

The right in-house processes. Confirm the shop actually owns the steps your part needs — cutting, forming, machining, welding and finishing — rather than quietly subcontracting them. In-house capability is what controls lead time and quality.

A real quality system. Look for a documented, audited system such as ISO 9001, plus inspection tools like a CMM for verifying critical dimensions. For government-adjacent work, a CAGE code (ours is 2W654) signals the shop is set up for traceable, spec-driven jobs.

Materials and capacity that fit. Make sure the shop routinely works your metal and has the equipment to scale — heavy press-brake tonnage, large-format laser cutting, plate rolling, robotic welding and large powder ovens all indicate it can carry both big parts and real volume.

Track record and responsiveness. An established shop — ours has been fabricating since 1982 — and a clear, fast quoting process are strong signals. If getting a straight answer to an RFQ is hard, running production will be harder.

RFQ

How to write a good RFQ.

The quality of your quote depends heavily on the quality of your request. A complete RFQ lets a shop price the job accurately the first time, instead of padding for unknowns or coming back with a list of questions. Include as much of the following as you can:

A drawing or CAD model. A dimensioned drawing (PDF) plus a 3D model (STEP) is ideal. Call out the tolerances that actually matter — over-specifying tight tolerances everywhere drives up cost for no benefit, so flag only the critical features.

Material and finish. Specify the metal and grade (for example 304 stainless or 6061 aluminum), the thickness or bar size, and the finish — powder-coat color, paint spec, or bare. If you’re open to a substitution that saves cost, say so.

Quantity and schedule. State the quantity for this order and any expected annual volume, plus when you need parts. Volume changes how a shop tools and prices the job, and a realistic date lets it plan capacity.

Application and standards. A sentence on what the part does, where it operates, and any codes or inspection requirements helps the shop suggest better material or design choices — and quote the right level of documentation. When your drawing and these details are in hand, send it in and you’ll get a real price and lead time back.

FAQ

Custom metal fabrication — answered.

What is custom metal fabrication?

Custom metal fabrication is the build-to-print process of turning raw metal — sheet, plate, tube and bar — into finished parts and assemblies through cutting, forming, machining, welding, finishing and assembly, engineered to your specification rather than pulled from a catalog.

What are the core metal fabrication processes?

The main processes are cutting (laser or plasma), forming and bending on a press brake, machining, welding, finishing such as powder coating, and final assembly. Most custom parts pass through several of these steps in sequence.

Which metals are used in custom fabrication?

The three most common are carbon steel for strength and value, stainless steel for corrosion resistance and hygiene, and aluminum for light weight. The right choice depends on load, environment, weight targets and finish.

What is the advantage of one-roof fabrication?

Keeping cutting, forming, machining, welding and finishing under one roof removes freight between vendors, shortens lead times, keeps one company accountable for quality, and lets engineering catch issues before parts are built.

How do I choose a metal fabrication partner?

Look for the right in-house processes for your part, a documented quality system such as ISO 9001, relevant materials experience, capacity to scale from prototype to production, and a clear, responsive quoting process.
