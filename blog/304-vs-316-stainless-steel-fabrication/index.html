304 vs. 316 stainless steel: which to fabricate with?.

The two most common stainless grades, where each belongs, and what the choice means for corrosion, cost, and weldability.

Fabrication · Jul 27, 2026 · 7 min read · Southern Perfection Fabrication, Byron, GA

Put a 304 and a 316 stainless part side by side and most people can't tell them apart. Same silvery finish, same feel, same weldable, formable, corrosion-resistant behavior on the bench. But the two grades don't age the same way. In the wrong environment, a 304 weldment can show tea-staining or pitting while an identical 316 part stays clean for years — and in the right environment, paying the 316 premium buys you nothing you'll ever notice.

For anyone specifying stainless for a fabricated part or weldment, the 304-vs-316 decision comes down to three levers: how corrosive the service environment is, how long the part has to last, and what you're willing to pay. This is a plain-English look at what actually separates the two grades and how to land on the right one without overspending.

What actually differs between the two grades

Both 304 and 316 are austenitic stainless steels built on the same chromium-nickel backbone, and that chromium is what gives stainless its self-healing passive oxide layer. The headline difference is a single added element: 316 contains roughly 2-3% molybdenum, which 304 does not (these are general metallurgical ranges, not lab data from a specific heat). Molybdenum sharpens the alloy's resistance to localized attack — pitting and crevice corrosion — particularly where chlorides are present. 316 also tends to carry slightly more nickel. That's essentially the whole story: same family, same look, one grade with a corrosion-resistance additive and a matching price tag.

You'll also see the low-carbon variants, 304L and 316L. The "L" lowers carbon content to reduce carbide precipitation at weld zones, which matters on heavier or heavily welded sections. For fabricated and welded assemblies, the L grades are a common and sensible default.

Corrosion resistance: where 316 pulls ahead

In ordinary indoor air, clean water, or dry conditions, 304 and 316 both resist corrosion well enough that you'd struggle to tell them apart over the life of the part. The gap opens up when chlorides enter the picture: coastal and marine air, road salt, swimming pools, brines, and many chemical-process streams. Chlorides are what break down the passive layer and start pits, and molybdenum is specifically what helps 316 shrug them off longer.

So the rule of thumb is qualitative but reliable: the saltier, wetter, or more chemically aggressive the environment, the more 316 earns its keep. Marine hardware, food and beverage wash-down zones, pharmaceutical and chemical equipment, and outdoor coastal installations are classic 316 territory. Dry indoor framework, brackets, enclosures, and architectural trim usually don't need it.

The cost premium of 316

316 costs more than 304 — typically a meaningful premium per pound driven mostly by the molybdenum and the higher nickel content, both of which track volatile commodity markets. On a small bracket the dollar difference is trivial; on a large weldment or a high-volume run it's a line item worth respecting. That's the whole reason the decision matters: if 316 were the same price, you'd simply spec it everywhere and move on. Because it isn't, the right question is always "does this environment actually need molybdenum?" rather than "which grade is better?"

Weldability and fabrication behavior

Good news for both: 304 and 316 are highly weldable and behave similarly under the torch. Our MIG and FANUC robotic welding stations run both grades routinely, as does hand TIG for finer or cosmetic work. The bigger fabrication story isn't which grade welds better — it's what happens to the stainless after welding. Heat from welding damages the passive layer and leaves oxidized "heat tint" near the weld, and that discolored zone is exactly where corrosion likes to start.

That's why cleanliness and post-weld treatment matter more than grade selection for real-world durability. Removing heat tint and restoring the chromium-oxide layer — through mechanical cleaning, pickling, or passivation — protects the investment, and it matters even more on 316 parts destined for aggressive service. Keeping stainless separate from carbon steel during fabrication also prevents iron contamination that can cause surface rust that looks like the stainless itself failed. Whether you're building a stainless steel weldment or precision custom metal parts, the finishing discipline is a big part of getting the corrosion life you paid for.

Machinability and forming

On the machining and forming side, 304 and 316 are close cousins. Both work-harden readily, both are gummier and more demanding than mild steel, and both reward sharp tooling, rigid setups, and correct feeds and speeds. 316 tends to be marginally tougher to machine, but in practice the difference is small compared to the gap between stainless and carbon steel. For cutting, our laser and plasma cutting handles both grades cleanly, and press-brake forming behaves similarly for each — mind the higher springback and generous bend radii that austenitic stainless calls for regardless of which grade you pick.

When 304 is plenty

304 is the workhorse of stainless fabrication for good reason. For indoor equipment, structural frames, enclosures, guards, brackets, food-contact parts that aren't in constant chloride wash-down, architectural elements, and general-purpose weldments, 304 delivers excellent corrosion resistance at the lower price. If the part lives in a dry or mild environment and never sees salt or aggressive chemistry, 316 is largely paying for protection the part will never use.

When 316 is worth it

316 earns its premium wherever chlorides, salt, or corrosive chemistry are part of daily life: marine and coastal installations, food and beverage processing with chloride sanitizers, pharmaceutical and chemical equipment, brine and wastewater handling, and anything exposed to road salt. It's also the safer call when a part is hard to inspect or replace, when downtime from corrosion is expensive, or when the customer's spec simply requires it. In those cases the extra cost is cheap insurance against premature pitting.

Don't overspecify — 316 isn't automatically the "safe" choice

It's tempting to default to 316 as the premium, can't-go-wrong option. Honestly, for most indoor and dry applications that's just spending money you don't need to. 304 is not a compromise grade — it's the correct answer for a huge share of fabricated parts, and defaulting to 316 across the board quietly inflates cost without adding real-world life to a bracket that lives in a climate-controlled room. Overspecifying also compounds on high-volume runs, where the per-pound premium multiplies across every part. The goal isn't the fanciest alloy; it's the right alloy for the environment the part actually lives in.

Finishing and appearance

Cosmetically, 304 and 316 are nearly indistinguishable and take the same range of finishes — from mill and brushed to polished. Neither grade "looks" more premium than the other, so appearance rarely drives the choice. Where finish does matter is corrosion performance: a uniform, clean, properly passivated surface resists attack better than a scratched or contaminated one, on either grade. If a part needs to stay bright in a demanding environment, the finishing and passivation work is often as important as the grade on the drawing.

What we see on the floor

Southern Perfection has run complete metal fabrication under one roof since 1982 in Byron, GA, and stainless — both 304 and 316 — moves through the shop regularly across cutting, forming, welding, and machining. The pattern we see most often isn't a grade problem; it's a finishing problem. Parts specified correctly but cleaned or passivated poorly after welding are the ones that come back with surface staining, and that's avoidable. Keeping stainless isolated from carbon steel, dressing out heat tint, and passivating where it counts is where corrosion life is won or lost.

We also see plenty of parts spec'd in 316 that would have been perfectly happy in 304, and the occasional 304 part quietly corroding in a chloride environment that called for 316. Our in-house SolidWorks design and engineering team, AWS-certified welders, and ISO 9001 quality system (CAGE 2W654) are all pointed at the same thing: getting the material and the finishing right the first time. If you're planning a stainless part or weldment, our custom manufacturing team can talk grade and finish before the first cut, and our custom metal fabrication guide covers the broader material and process decisions.

Tell us the environment your part lives in and we'll help you land on 304 or 316 — without overspending.

Frequently asked questions

Is 316 stainless always better than 304?

No. 316 resists chlorides and salt better because of its added molybdenum, but in dry or mild environments 304 performs just as well for far less money. "Better" depends entirely on the environment the part lives in.

Can you tell 304 and 316 apart by looking?

Not reliably — they have the same appearance and take the same finishes. The difference is chemical, not visual, which is why mill certs and proper material control matter during fabrication.

Do 304 and 316 weld differently?

Both weld well and behave similarly. The more important factor is post-weld cleaning and passivation: heat tint left near the weld is where corrosion starts, so restoring the passive layer protects either grade.

Which grade should I use for outdoor or coastal parts?

For coastal, marine, or road-salt exposure, 316 is usually the better call because its molybdenum resists chloride pitting. For sheltered outdoor or dry indoor use, 304 is typically sufficient.

How much more does 316 cost than 304?

316 carries a meaningful per-pound premium driven by molybdenum and higher nickel, and both track volatile commodity prices. On small parts the difference is minor; on large weldments or high-volume runs it becomes a line item worth confirming against the actual environment.
